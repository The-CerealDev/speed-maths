\noindent\textit{Each question pairs two techniques from different days.}

\begin{enumerate}

\item Prove by contrapositive: ``If $n$ is not a multiple of $3$ and not a multiple of $5$, then $n$ is not a multiple of $15$.''

\item Prove by contradiction: ``There is no positive integer $n$ such that $n^2+n$ is odd.''

\item Prove by induction: $\sum_{i=1}^n i^2=\dfrac{n(n+1)(2n+1)}{6}$ for all positive integers $n$.

\item A student wants to prove ``if $n$ is not a multiple of $5$, then $n^2$ is not a multiple of $25$'' by instead showing ``if $n$ is a multiple of $5$, then $n^2$ is a multiple of $25$.'' Is this a valid proof of the original claim? Justify your answer.

\item Give a counterexample showing that ``$n$ is prime'' is not sufficient for ``$n$ is odd.''

\item Negate the statement: ``For every set $A$, there exists a set $B$ such that $A\cap B=\emptyset$.'' Is the negated statement true or false?

\item Prove by induction that $n^3+2n$ is divisible by $3$ for all positive integers $n$.

\item Prove by contradiction that $\sqrt6$ is irrational.

\item For a polynomial $f$, classify ``$f(x)>0$ for all real $x$'' as necessary, sufficient, both, or neither for ``$f$ has no real roots.''

\item A student proves $P(n)$ for all $n\geq1$ by checking $P(1)$ directly, then showing $P(k)\implies P(k+1)$ only for $k\geq2$ (skipping $k=1$). Explain the gap this leaves in the proof.

\end{enumerate}

\section*{Section C \quad Substitution \& Structure \hfill{\normalfont\small($\leq$90\,s each)}}
% ══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

\noindent\textit{Tricky synthesis MCQs, combining techniques from across the week.}

\begin{enumerate}

\item Let $P$: ``$n$ is divisible by $4$'' and $Q$: ``$n$ is divisible by $2$ and $n$ is divisible by $6$''. Which of the following is true?
\begin{itemize}
\item[A)] $P\iff Q$.
\item[B)] $P$ is sufficient but not necessary for $Q$.
\item[C)] $P$ is necessary but not sufficient for $Q$.
\item[D)] $P$ and $Q$ are neither necessary nor sufficient for each other.
\item[E)] $Q$ is impossible to satisfy.
\end{itemize}

\item A theorem states: ``If every element of a finite set $S$ of positive integers is even, then the sum of all elements of $S$ is even.'' A student, wanting to prove the converse, argues: ``Take $S=\{2,4\}$; the sum is $6$, which is even, and every element is indeed even, confirming the converse.'' What is the flaw?
\begin{itemize}
\item[A)] The theorem itself is false.
\item[B)] A single supporting example does not prove a universal converse claim.
\item[C)] $S=\{2,4\}$ does not actually have an even sum.
\item[D)] The original theorem's proof technique (not the converse) is what's flawed.
\item[E)] There is no flaw; the converse is proved.
\end{itemize}

\item Which proof technique is most directly suited to establishing: ``There exists a positive integer $n$ such that $n^2+3n+1$ is not prime''?
\begin{itemize}
\item[A)] Induction.
\item[B)] Direct exhibition of a specific such $n$ (e.g.\ $n=6$: $6^2+3(6)+1=55=5\times11$).
\item[C)] Contradiction.
\item[D)] Contrapositive.
\item[E)] Strong induction.
\end{itemize}

\item A student claims: ``$n$ is a multiple of $9$'' is necessary and sufficient for ``the digit sum of $n$ is a multiple of $9$''. They verify this only for $n=9,18,27$ and declare the claim proved. Evaluate this.
\begin{itemize}
\item[A)] The claim and the verification are both fully correct.
\item[B)] Checking finitely many cases cannot prove a universal claim.
\item[C)] The claim is false; digit sums have nothing to do with divisibility by $9$.
\item[D)] The claim is true only for necessity, not sufficiency.
\item[E)] The claim is true only for sufficiency, not necessity.
\end{itemize}

\item What is the contrapositive of ``If $n^2$ is not divisible by $4$, then $n$ is odd''?
\begin{itemize}
\item[A)] If $n$ is odd, then $n^2$ is not divisible by $4$.
\item[B)] If $n$ is even, then $n^2$ is divisible by $4$.
\item[C)] If $n^2$ is divisible by $4$, then $n$ is even.
\item[D)] If $n$ is not odd, then $n^2$ is not divisible by $4$.
\item[E)] If $n^2$ is divisible by $4$, then $n$ is odd.
\end{itemize}

\item A student claims $P(n)$: ``$n^2+5n+2$ is even for every positive integer $n$'' is provable by induction: base case $P(1)$: $1+5+2=8$ is even; inductive step: assume $k^2+5k+2$ is even; then $(k+1)^2+5(k+1)+2=k^2+7k+8=(k^2+5k+2)+2k+6$, which is (even)+(even)=even, so $P(k+1)$ holds. Is this induction valid, and is the underlying claim true?
\begin{itemize}
\item[A)] The induction is valid, and the claim is true for every positive integer.
\item[B)] The induction has a flaw in the algebra.
\item[C)] The claim is false --- there exists some $n$ for which $n^2+5n+2$ is odd.
\item[D)] The base case is wrong.
\end{itemize}

\item Consider positive integers $m,n$. Statement $P$: ``For every prime $p$, if $p\mid mn$ then $p\mid m$ or $p\mid n$.'' Statement $Q$: ``There exists a prime $p$ such that $p\mid mn$ but $p\nmid m$ and $p\nmid n$.'' Which of the following is true?
\begin{itemize}
\item[A)] $P$ and $Q$ can both be true simultaneously.
\item[B)] $Q$ is the negation of $P$, so $Q$ is always false because $P$ is always true.
\item[C)] $P$ is the negation of $Q$.
\item[D)] Neither $P$ nor $Q$ is ever true.
\item[E)] $P$ is false in general.
\end{itemize}

\item A student claims: ``$x=1$'' is necessary and sufficient for ``$x^3-1=0$'' (for real $x$), reasoning: ``Sufficient, because substituting $x=1$ gives $0$. Necessary, because $1^3-1=0$, which confirms $x=1$ works.'' Evaluate the necessity argument specifically.
\begin{itemize}
\item[A)] Both parts of the reasoning are fully correct as given.
\item[B)] The ``necessity'' argument is circular: showing $1^3-1=0$ just re-verifies sufficiency again, and says nothing about whether $x=1$ is the ONLY solution.
\item[C)] The necessity argument is flawed because $x=-1$ is also a solution.
\item[D)] Sufficiency is actually false.
\item[E)] There is no correct way to establish necessity here.
\end{itemize}

\end{enumerate}

\section*{Section D \quad Challenge \hfill{\normalfont\small(SMC / BMO1 difficulty)}}
% ══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

\noindent\textit{Full capstone proofs, drawing on every technique from the week.}

\begin{enumerate}

\item Long John Silverman has buried treasure at an integer point $(x,y)$ (not necessarily positive). He records the two values $x^2+y$ and $x+y^2$; these two recorded numbers are distinct from each other. Prove that no other integer point $(x',y')\neq(x,y)$ can produce the same pair of recorded values (i.e.\ satisfy both $x'^2+y'=x^2+y$ and $x'+y'^2=x+y^2$) --- so the map pins down the treasure's location uniquely. \textit{\small(after BMO1 2010 Q6)}

\item Two bags contain $m$ and $n$ balls respectively ($m,n>0$). Two operations are allowed, applied any number of times in any order: (a) remove the same number of balls from each bag; (b) triple the number of balls in one (chosen) bag. \textit{\small(after BMO1 2012 Q4)}\\
Prove that if $m-n$ is odd, it is impossible to ever empty both bags (i.e.\ reach $(0,0)$) using only these two operations.

\item Prove that there do not exist positive integers $a<b<c$ with $\dfrac1a+\dfrac1b+\dfrac1c=1$ and $a,b,c$ all even.

\item Prove, using strong induction, that every positive integer $n\geq2$ can be written as a product of primes in exactly one way, up to the order of the factors (the uniqueness part of the Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic) --- assuming as given Euclid's Lemma: if a prime $p$ divides a product $ab$, then $p\mid a$ or $p\mid b$.

\item Consider the statement $(*)$: ``For all positive integers $m,n$: $m$ and $n$ are coprime if and only if there exist integers $x,y$ (not necessarily positive) with $mx+ny=1$'' (Bézout's Identity).\\
(a) Prove the $\Longleftarrow$ direction of $(*)$ directly.\\
(b) Taking the $\implies$ direction of $(*)$ as given (it is normally proved via the Euclidean algorithm), use $(*)$ to prove: if $p$ is prime and $p\nmid a$, then there exist integers $x,y$ with $px+ay=1$; hence deduce Euclid's Lemma (if $p\mid ab$ then $p\mid a$ or $p\mid b$) as a consequence.

\end{enumerate}
